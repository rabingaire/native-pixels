11.1Choose a position in useful units#

The indexed rectangle is visible, but its coordinates are fractions of the output area. We want to say that it starts at (120,100) and is 48 units wide by 64 units tall.

We will define local corners from zero to one, then calculate world and normalized device coordinates on the CPU. For this chapter only, one world unit means one framebuffer pixel. A fresh copy of the local corners will be transformed each frame, so the calculation never transforms its own previous result.

11.2The problem with moving in NDC#

An NDC x distance of one spans half the window width; an NDC y distance of one spans half its height. In a wide window those distances represent different pixel counts. Defining a character as “0.2 by 0.2” therefore does not define a square on screen. NDC is useful as the pipeline’s common coordinate language, but it is inconvenient as our game’s measurement system.

We want an upper-left position of (120,100), a width of 48, and a height of 64. Begin with local corners (0,0), (1,0), (1,1), (0,1). A local coordinate describes a point relative to the character itself. Multiplying by size gives a displacement from its origin; adding position puts it in the world. For now the world is measured in framebuffer pixels, and its origin is the window’s top-left.

11.3Derive the conversion rather than memorize it#

For x, dividing pixel_x by the width W maps the left and right boundaries to 0 and 1. Multiplying by two maps them to 0 and 2. Subtracting one produces −1 and +1. For y, dividing by height H also gives 0 at the top and 1 at the bottom. We want +1 at the top and −1 at the bottom, so use one minus twice that fraction.

Pixel position in 960 × 640NDCInterpretation
(0,0)(−1,+1)Upper-left image boundary
(480,320)(0,0)Image center
(960,640)(+1,−1)Lower-right boundary
(120,100)(−0.75,+0.6875)Character upper-left
(168,164)(−0.65,+0.4875)Character lower-right

Width and height appear independently in the formula, which corrects aspect ratio without a separate aspect-ratio multiplier. A 48-pixel horizontal span maps to a different NDC span than a 48-pixel vertical span in a nonsquare window, and that is exactly what keeps the displayed dimensions correct. No matrix is needed to express scale, translation, and this projection.

The constant VERTICES remains local geometry. Each frame starts with a fresh copy and transforms that copy. Transforming the already transformed previous-frame positions would accumulate scale and translation and quickly send the rectangle off screen. The local data and the projected output have different meanings even though both use two floating-point components.

The buffer still has CopyDst usage from its original upload, so updating it is permitted. We overwrite its 96 bytes with projected positions and original colors. Queue ordering places that write before the submitted draw. The vertex shader still returns its input position with z=0 and w=1. The GPU therefore sees exactly the NDC values we computed on the CPU.

11.5Pixels, edges, and clipping#

The character’s upper-left position describes a boundary, not a pixel center. Integer-aligned edges at (120,100) and (168,164) cover the expected interior sample centers for an unrotated rectangle. Subpixel movement can move an edge between those centers, and a single-sampled hard-edged shape can appear to step between coverage changes. Frame-rate-independent motion does not guarantee antialiased edges. We intentionally do not add multisampling to address that separate topic.

A negative world x moves part of the character outside the window; the normal clip process trims the visible triangles. We do not need CPU-side clipping to keep GPU output within the attachment. Later we will clamp game position so the user can keep the character visible, which is a gameplay choice rather than a rendering safety requirement.

This chapter uses framebuffer pixels intentionally. Once high-density windows are enabled, we will choose SDL logical window units for game measurements and retain physical pixels for surface configuration. The formula remains the same, but W and H will refer to the logical world extent. That change will be explicit, not a silent reinterpretation of position.

11.12Challenges#

Optional extensions. The complete game in chapter 23 requires none of these. Work in a separate copy if you want to explore; the next chapter starts from the unmodified result described here.

  1. Anchor a marker at its center

    Define a center position for a rectangular marker and derive its top-left position by subtracting half its size. Keep the GPU conversion unchanged.

    Check your result: Increasing the marker’s size leaves its center in the same world position.

  2. Add a translation camera

    Subtract a camera position from each world corner before projecting. Move the camera using two keys while keeping the character’s world position fixed.

    Check your result: Moving the camera right moves the character left on screen by the corresponding amount.
